Yellowstone is a masterclass in patience, requiring you to sit through massive, bison-induced traffic jams just to stand on a freezing wooden boardwalk and watch the earth boil over.

The entire park smells like a blown-out match. You roll down the window, expecting crisp mountain air, and get hit directly in the face by heavy, cooking sulfur.

Yellowstone operates like an active warning sign. You walk out on the wooden boardwalks past massive, terraced pools of neon orange and scalding blue. It looks like a hostile, alien planet, complete with skeletal pine trees entombed in white chalk. Just raw, exposed geology. You stay on the path because a wrong step into the crust means you dissolve.

Navigating the park requires sharing the road with two-ton roadblocks. You will sit in an I-guess-we-live-here-now backup for an hour just to watch a lone bison methodically chew grass in the oncoming lane. But once you finally clear the traffic, the scale of the place breaks open. The river cuts a massive, yellow-walled canyon straight through the dense forests, dropping into waterfalls that kick up enough mist to completely soak the rim.

The weather shifts constantly. A heavy, cold fog rolls in and mixes with the steam rising off the giant prismatic springs, turning the basin into a hazy, neon-ringed sauna. Geysers erupt into the clouds, throwing boiling water high over the scrubland and fallen timber.

Earning the quiet moments requires outlasting the tour buses. We waited for the sun to drop and drove up to the north entrance. The crowds finally vanished. We parked under the massive stone of the Roosevelt Arch, stood shivering in the absolute dark, and watched the Milky Way burn over the valley.
