Early January in Joshua Tree is a freezing, wind-whipped maze of stacked granite and spiked vegetation that actively forces you to scramble for a view before the early winter sunset drops the temperature back to zero.

The landscape looks like a glitch in the geography. Massive piles of smooth, rounded granite just sit scattered across the flat sand. Everything growing in the basin is heavily armed. The Joshua trees shoot out of the dirt in erratic, unpredictable angles, holding dense clusters of sharp green spikes against the flat blue sky. Even the dead timber looks hostile. Dark, twisted branches lay sprawled over the boulders, bleached and hardened by the dry air.

Navigating the park requires abandoning the paved trails and using your hands. You pick a ridge of stacked granite and start pulling yourself up by cold, scraped fingertips. It is a highly specific, I-hope-I-don't-snap-an-ankle-in-this-crevice kind of scrambling. But gaining elevation pays out. The horizon opens up, revealing an endless stretch of brush and rock sprawling out toward the distant mountains.

The winter daylight is aggressively short. By late afternoon, the sun drops low over the western ridges, turning the sky into a massive, fading gradient of pale yellow and deep blue. The harsh midday glare softens, throwing the giant yucca into stark, jagged silhouettes against the hills. The temperature immediately plummets. We sat on the rocks just long enough to watch the light fade.
