When you spend enough time driving across the flat, sun-baked expanse of West Texas, your brain gets used to a certain kind of emptiness. Just miles of yuccas dotting the sandy earth beneath a massive blue sky swept with wispy clouds. It's quiet. Hypnotic, almost. Then, without warning, a towering wall of ancient limestone violently thrusts itself out of the desert floor.

The ruggedness of Guadalupe Mountains National Park doesn't ease you into its ruggedness. It just sits there, looming over the scrub brush, silently challenging you to come any closer.

Since I am a landscape photographer, my usual aim is to find the ideal combination of vast scale and a quiet sense of intimacy. Over here, there's no need to search hard; the beauty of this desert is not soft; it is something you achieve. You can feel it in the dust that has built up on your boots and in the continuous wind blowing over the ridges.

You put the Canon away and begin the climb, following the dusty tracks bordered by dry, tough vegetation. From time to time you come across the twisted, sun-bleached skeleton of a dead tree standing by the path—just the kind of weathered foreground feature that directs the eye towards the faraway, shadowy slopes. Although it's a severe environment, the textures are wonderful. The deep grooves in the dead wood and the way the light strikes the huge, steep cliff faces turn the rough stone into a canvas of gold and strong contrast. Of course, reaching the high-elevation viewpoints involves giving a tremendous amount of sweat. The mountain lays on you a continuous series of switchbacks, offering you false summits while the canyon walls appear to go on infinitely. My legs definitely felt the pain.

But then you reach the roof of Texas.

Standing at the edge of the world, looking down past a massive, jagged fin of rock jutting over the abyss, the scale of it all finally clicks. Below, the vast, arid desert floor stretches out, bleeding softly into the hazy horizon. Up there, with the wind roaring in your ears and your shutter clicking away, the exhaustion evaporates. It's not just a beautiful view. It's a beautifully unforgiving one. And honestly, I wouldn't want to shoot it any other way.
