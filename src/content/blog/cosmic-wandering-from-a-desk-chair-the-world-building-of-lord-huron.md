I don't remember exactly when Lord Huron crossed the line from "band I casually listen to" to "the official soundtrack of my internal monologue." Probably somewhere on a long stretch of highway, or maybe during a quiet editing session when the house was completely still.

It's approaching midnight on a Thursday. The cool gray interface of Lightroom Classic is the only thing glowing in the room. I’m staring at a stack of sixty raw moon images, trying to coax out the subtle mineral colors buried in the pixels. It's a tedious, zoom-in-to-300-percent-and-stare process. The kind of work that requires you to shut out the rest of the world entirely.

So, I grab my headphones. And almost by default, I queue up _Strange Trails_.

If you’ve never really listened to Lord Huron, it’s easy to write them off as just another indie band with a penchant for reverb and acoustic guitars. But if you actually pay attention, you realize they aren't just writing songs. They’re world-building.

Ben Schneider and the rest of the band have somehow managed to bottle a very specific, haunting atmosphere. It's music for wandering. Their albums are heavily soaked in themes of cosmic insignificance, restless ghosts, dying stars, and the endless, unforgiving expanse of the American West. It’s the sonic equivalent of standing alone in the dark at an overlook in Rocky Mountain National Park, staring up at a sky so dense with stars that you suddenly feel incredibly small.

As a landscape and astrophotographer, I’m constantly chasing that feeling.

There is a strange, magnetic pull to the unknown. Whether I'm standing behind my Canon R7 in the freezing chill of a night shoot waiting for a long exposure to finish, or staring at a blank Scrivener document trying to hammer out the next scene of a novel, Lord Huron provides the perfect frequency. Their music bridges the gap between the grounded dirt under our boots and the massive, uncaring universe swirling above us. A stare-into-the-abyss kind of soundtrack.

Of course, the grand, sweeping romance of cosmic wandering hits a little differently when you’re just sitting in a desk chair in Murphysboro, Illinois.

The epic journey usually pauses when my eyes finally blur from staring at the monitor for too long. I pull the headphones off. The soaring cinematic strings and twangy guitars instantly vanish, replaced by the low, steady hum of the refrigerator in the kitchen. The vast, starry wilderness gets put on hold so I can wake up tomorrow and write software documentation.

But the edits look good. The moon finally has its color. I hit save, shut down the computer, and walk down the dark hallway to bed.
