I am, first and foremost, a student of the landscape and the sky. Put a person in front of my lens, and I suddenly forget how to frame a shot—portraits and human subjects are genuinely hard for me. But give me a sweeping valley, a shifting sand dune, or a dark sky reserve, and I know exactly what I am looking for. 

Over the years, dragging a camera bag up mountains and through national parks, I have refined my setup to ensure I never miss a shot while carrying only the gear I need. 

Here is a breakdown of my current kit, the optical sweet spots for each lens, and how to approach building a "covers all bases" setup of your own.

## The Core Foundation

**The Body: Canon EOS R7**
For landscape and wildlife, the APS-C sensor on the R7 is a massive advantage. The 1.6x crop factor gives every lens more reach, letting me compress distant mountain layers without carrying massive, heavy glass. The in-body image stabilization (IBIS) is incredibly reliable when I am forced to shoot handheld in fading light. 

**The Legs: Falcam TreeRoot Tripod**
A landscape photographer is only as good as their tripod. The TreeRoot carbon fiber tripod is lightweight enough for day hikes but stable enough to prevent micro-jitters during 15-second Milky Way exposures. The twist-lock joints and modular center column mean I can set it up in seconds, even in challenging terrain.

## The Arsenal

**The Wide Angle: Sigma 10-18mm f/2.8 DC DN**
When you are standing inside a canyon or under a massive structure, a standard zoom simply isn't wide enough. This lens is incredibly compact, and the f/2.8 aperture is fast enough for blue hour shooting. It is my go-to for capturing the sheer scale of an environment when stepping back isn't an option.

*   **The Sweet Spot:** The image center is excellent straight from f/2.8, though the corners at the 18mm end can be slightly soft wide open. Diffraction effects start to kick in from f/8, and f/11 should already be avoided. Staying between f/4 and f/5.6 will give you the cleanest edge-to-edge landscape shots.

**The Travel Workhorse: Canon RF-S 18-150mm f/3.5-6.3 IS STM**
If I am doing a lot of rock scrambling and only want to carry one lens, this is it. It covers almost every focal length you need for daytime hiking. It is light, versatile, and stays on my camera 70% of the time when I am exploring a new trail and don't know exactly what I will encounter.

*   **The Sweet Spot:** This lens is a solid, versatile performer, but its true sweet spot for sharpness is between 50 and 90mm. 

**The Specialty Glass: Sigma 17-40mm f/1.8 DC Art**
This is a masterpiece for APS-C mirrorless cameras, giving me a bag of fast primes in a single zoom. The constant f/1.8 aperture makes it my absolute go-to for capturing the Milky Way and dark sky scenes.

*   **The Sweet Spot:** This lens is an absolute beast. It delivers tack-sharp, corner-to-corner clarity even wide open at f/1.8. It gives you the dramatic light-gathering power needed for astrophotography without forcing you to stop down to recover edge detail, though shooting at f/2.8 or f/4 will yield a slight bump in global contrast for daytime landscapes.

**The Telephoto: Canon RF 100-400mm f/5.6-8 IS USM**
Telephoto lenses aren’t just for wildlife; they are incredible for isolating light on a distant ridge or compressing the layers of shifting dunes. Because it has a relatively slow aperture, it is remarkably small and light for a 400mm equivalent reach. And when it comes to astrophotography, this is the lens I mount on my tripod to capture the moon's high-resolution, mineral colors.

*   **The Sweet Spot:** At 100mm f/5.6, the corners are slightly soft, but they recover to very good quality at f/8 already. The f/8 to f/11 range is widely considered the sweet spot for this lens, especially at longer focal lengths. When pushed all the way to 400mm (where the lens is wide open at f/8), stopping down to f/10 provides peak center sharpness.

## General Guidelines for Building Your Setup

If you are just starting out, do not fall into the trap of buying gear just to have it. Build your kit methodically:

*   **Mind the Gap (But Not Too Much):** Try to cover the ultra-wide (10-18mm), standard (18-50mm), and telephoto (70-200mm+) ranges. It is okay if there are small gaps between your focal lengths; you can always crop in or take a step forward.
*   **Prioritize Weight for Field Work:** An f/2.8 telephoto zoom is visually stunning, but if it weighs three pounds and ruins your back on a hike, you will end up leaving it in the hotel room. Buy the lens you will actually carry.
*   **Invest in One "Specialty" Lens:** Identify your absolute favorite type of photography—whether it is astrophotography, macro, or low-light street photography—and invest in one fast, high-quality lens dedicated solely to that craft. 
*   **Don't Skimp on the Tripod:** A cheap, flimsy tripod will blur your images in a light breeze and ultimately cost you more when you inevitably have to replace it. Buy a sturdy carbon fiber travel tripod from the start.
